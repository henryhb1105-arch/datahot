Part Four - Learnings at LinkedIn Scale

I know I went a little deeper into the inference side of things. I'm going to keep it really simple in the last four slides. It's pretty simple. These are the major learnings that you could take away from this talk and from our work. The first one is what I call benchmark what you serve. You will be like, "Sundara, that's so obvious. What are you doing otherwise?" I will tell a small story of what we did. We do all these beautiful optimizations in model, context, inference, and then we go and do an offline benchmark. We figure out the throughput we were able to get out of a GPU and then we know how much QPS we want to serve. We do the estimates, and we go inform our leadership, this is the amount of GPUs we need. They were happy and we were happy they were happy.

We go deploy this in production and we send some dark traffic, that's where we see an entirely different result. We were not able to realize the offline benchmark numbers we were able to get. We are scratching our head like, what is wrong here? Is there anything wrong with the hardware? Is there anything wrong with the specific GPU, or is there any communication bottleneck between the client and server? Something happening over the network. It turned out, we were doing our benchmarks incorrectly. When we are simulating synthetic traffic, we were effectively using even distribution to send the request. What it means is within a second interval, you are estimating based on QPS. You're going to say that my throughput is 1000 jobs per second, 1000 items per second, or something of that sort. When you're simulating the synthetic traffic, the requests are getting sent in an equally spaced manner.

All of them arrive in a uniform distribution with equal spaces. In production, when you're serving real time user traffic, you cannot expect the users to follow the same patterns. Within a second interval, you might have a surge in requests so the queue depth increases. After that, there could be some quieter periods. The major takeaway from this here is if you are serving systems in production, the closest possible result you can get is if you can record and replay your production traffic. If you don't have that option, the second closest thing you could get is if you can simulate the synthetic traffic using a Poisson distribution instead of an even distribution. Just make sure you're doing the right thing there. The second learning is what I call offline must match online. What do we mean by this? When you're working with a model-based system in production, there is an engineer who's going to work on fine-tuning the model.

There'll be prompt engineers who are designing the prompts, and they have their own set of code. The system that gets deployed in production is entirely different. You might be leveraging an open-source system, or you might be leveraging your own model server. There are a set of defaults which is different from both these. Like for example, a simple tokenization config like add end of sequence or beginning of sequence config, can change the entire prompt in itself. You have to make sure there is absolute parity between the offline and the online system. Don't discover all these disparities or discrepancies in production. Disparity has to be a gate without which you cannot launch in production at all.

The third learning is the crux of the talk. There is no single fix here when you are serving systems in production, models, LLMs, anything of that sort. You have to squeeze in throughput from all the possible places. You have to make the model as small as possible, and you have to make the context as short as possible and make the inference as efficient as possible. The final learning, if there are any tech leads, managers, and leaders, this is for you guys. When you are dealing with production systems, there are multiple actors here. You have the inference engineers. You have the model engineers. You have the prompt engineers. You have the product managers who are defining the product policies. Participant 1: Can you tell me more about that single token embedding?

Sundara Raman Ramachandran: The idea here is you can use LLMs as a cross encoder. What it essentially means is you can give full text and then get one single embedding representation out of it. There are multiple techniques how you get this embedding. You can do some last token pooling is what they call. You make the model to do a complete forward pass and then just take the embedding from the last token. We expect the last token to have the complete context of the entire full text. That is one way. The other way is you can take the embedding for each token and then do an average of all these embeddings to get the average template. There are multiple techniques out there. What worked for us is the last token embedding. We take the full text, give it to an LLM, use it as an encoding model, and then get the one single job embedding representation.

Effectively, if you think about it, like from 2000 tokens, this has become one token. In LLM systems internally, this is how it works. You give a list of tokens, let's say 100 tokens. All this first gets converted into one embedding for each of these 100 tokens. The embedding is the input to the overall matrix multiplication that goes into the model. Then it's finally going to spit out another embedding, and then you convert this embedding back, map it to another token, and that becomes your output. Embedding is the one which is the input to the matrix and everything is multiplied between.
