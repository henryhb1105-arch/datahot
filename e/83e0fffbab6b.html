Act III - The Human Cost

A few words around architecture are going to bounce off that. Junior engineers won't spend years reading legacy codebases or debugging production incidents at 3:00 in the morning.

Act IV - The Case Study

I've shown you a lot of graphs, but I think a recent problem presents us with a rather elegant case study on what's going wrong, the Amazon incidents. Who's heard of what's going on with Amazon right now? Yes, it's interesting. In mid-December 2025, AWS engineers allowed Amazon's Kiro AI coding tool to make changes to AWS Cost Explorer. The AI agent, given operator-level permissions equivalent to a human engineer, decided the best approach was to delete and recreate the entire environment rather than make targeted changes. AWS spent 13 hours recovering Cost Explorer in one of two China regions. Amazon called it user error, specifically, misconfigured access controls. Four sources later told the Financial Times that Kiro autonomously made that decision. A human engineer, one would hope, would know that delete and recreate the entire operational production environment is not a sane response to a configuration change. That knowledge comes from having seen things go wrong, from having lived through production incidents, from having absorbed the implicit rule that you don't blow away state in a production customer-facing environment.

AI had the permissions. It lacked the battle scars. Then, interestingly, came a series of outages on the Amazon retail site. Over 22,000 people reported issues during a roughly six-hour disruption on the 5th of March. Both the FT and the CNBC reported the internal briefing documents identified GenAI-assisted changes as the contributing factor. With a trend of incidents characterized by a high blast radius, a novel GenAI usage for which best practices and safeguards are not yet fully established. Five days later, on the 10th of March, SVP, Dave Treadwell, made normally optional, This Week in Stores Tech, TWiST meeting mandatory. His email apparently began, "Folks, as you likely know, the availability of the site and related infrastructure has not been good recently." Multiple news outlets then reported that Amazon had introduced new approval requirements. Junior and mid-level engineers were now required to seek senior sign-off on AI-assisted production changes.

Like that METR study, the story went around pretty much every engineering Slack channel in the world, some of you have come across it. However, interestingly, in the last few days, Amazon has really pushed back against this narrative. In a public statement, they said the FT reporting contained inaccuracies. Their version was that only one of the recent retail incidents involved AI tooling in any way. That incident was not caused by AI-written code. Instead, it was an engineer following inaccurate advice that an AI tool inferred from an outdated internal wiki. The other outages were separate, unrelated operational issues with no AI involvement whatsoever. No new approval requirements for engineers using AI tools had been introduced. Amazon characterized the AI-involved incident as human error, not AI failure. Amazon says that most of these outages have nothing to do with AI. Maybe they're right. I'm quite willing to believe them.

Look carefully at what Amazon did confirm. An AI tool read an outdated internal wiki. It inferred advice from stale documentation. An engineer followed that advice. The site went down. That's not a story about AI writing bad code, it's a story about context. The AI couldn't distinguish current wiki page from an obsolete one. It couldn't assess whether the advice it was synthesizing still applied to the systems as it exists today. It treated everything in its context window with equal weight because that's what these tools do. The engineer who followed the advice either didn't have the experience to question it or trusted the tool's confidence, and we all know how confident those models can be, far more than their own uncertainty.

As I said at the start of this talk, the problem was never writing code, it was always about what surrounds the code. The design decisions. The system context. The accumulated understanding of why things are the way they are. An outdated wiki page is a failure of institutional memory. The engineer who follows its advice without questioning it is a failure of judgment. Both of these failures map directly to missing rungs. A reliance on AI rather than your own judgment. An arXiv paper from last month tracked how teams manage AI context. One project evolved into 26,000 lines of codified context, more instructions than actual code. Meanwhile, an analysis by GitClear of 211 million lines of code showed AI tools adoption increasing code volume by 10%, but collapsing quality metrics, refactoring dropped by 60%. Copy and paste rose by 48%. Code churn jumped by 44%. The models writing code with less understanding of what already existed.

This is the distinction between prompt engineering, how you ask, and what we now call context engineering. What the model sees as it starts working. The teams that get this right, treat context like infrastructure, files that read like onboarding documents, memory hierarchies with progressive disclosures, architectural decisions recorded with reasoning behind them. The 26,000 lines of context architecture is not overhead, it's the institutional knowledge that used to live in people's heads, made explicit so that AI can use it too. New engineers can learn from it. Because most engineering is not greenfield, it's brownfield or what some people have started calling blackfield. Legacy systems under high load, on a deprecation path everyone agrees on but literally no one has time to execute. Been there. Features still being bolted on because the business can't wait. In blackfield systems the spec was never written or was written and made totally irrelevant by decades of undocumented decisions.

Business rules are encoded in conditions that outlived everyone who understood them. AI agents can read the code, tests and documentation but what they can't do is read production. They can't look at years of request patterns and understand which code paths are load bearing in ways the code themselves does not reveal. They can't distinguish a dead edge case from one that runs for 0.1% of your users, every single one of whom will immediately complain as soon as it disappears.

Amazon's outdated wiki is just the most visible form of this problem. Every codebase is full of implicit knowledge that hasn't been written down or perhaps worse yet was written down once and never updated. The AI can read what's there, it can't know what's missing or stale. Only someone who's lived with the system can do that. The honest sequence for non-greenfield systems isn't write spec, generate code, run tests. It's, observe production, extract behavioral contracts, encode the system tests, use that for the spec, and then bring in the agent. The tools for this already exist, characterization tests, contract testing, traffic shadowing, canary analysis. These aren't new ideas by any means. They're what software engineers worked out before AI entered the picture. AI conversation needs to inherit them rather than pretend the problem is being solved by better prompting. Whether you believe the FT's version or Amazon's version, the structural point is the same.

AWS Kiro incident, delete and recreate, is what happens when AI agents have permissions without judgment. Judgment that comes from years of production experience. Confirmed retail incident, the outdated wiki, is what happens when context isn't curated. Context curation is exactly the kind of institutional knowledge that the missing rungs were supposed to build. The dispute itself reveals how organizations resist framing these as structural problems. Amazon called it user error and routine operational review. In engineering, following confidently wrong AI advice from stale documentation is not a one-off mistake. It's a pattern that will reoccur everywhere AI tools are deployed into systems with institutional memory gaps, which pretty much is every large corporate system ever. The question isn't whether Amazon's outages were caused by AI. The question is what happens when AI tools are deployed into environments where the context they need, the production knowledge, the institutional memory, the understanding of why things are the way they are, lives in the human heads that were built by decades of doing the work AI now handles. Where does that context come from when the people who would have built it never did the work in the first place?

Act V - Mind the Gap

I'm not going to try and give you a part answer. As an industry, we are facing a fundamental structural problem. The ladder is missing rungs. Of course, the interesting thing is we've been here before. In the 1980s, the home computer revolution created a generation of engineers who learned by tinkering, my generation. We were brought up in the BBC Micro, the Commodore 64, the ZX Spectrum, the Trash-80, machines that booted to a basic prompt or CPM if you were really lucky. Kids wrote games, tools, experiments, mostly rubbish, but we learned. By the early 2000s, computers had become sealed appliances. Desktops booted into Windows, not to a prompt. Games came on disks, then over the internet. They definitely did not come with source code anymore. The Xbox replaced the BBC Micro in most homes. The ability for kids to write at home in their bedroom a game or an application that was just as good as a professional software house could.

That's something that happened a lot in the 1980s. A lot of the games sold in cassette tapes to kids like me were written by other kids in their bedrooms. That was now gone. Then by the mid-2000s, computer science departments started noticing that incoming students had never written code before coming up to university. They'd used computers their whole lives, but had never programmed one. They didn't understand how they worked. This started a mass panic in both academia and industry. There were a whole bunch of initiatives designed to deal with the problem. One of the more prominent ones was Raspberry Pi. Raspberry Pi was an explicit response, a deliberate recreation of accessible tinkering. Not, we need a cheap computer. That was the solution. The problem was, how do we get kids access to a machine they can own completely, that it didn't matter if it broke? One that boots to a prompt that had GPIO pins to allow them to touch the physical world.

It worked. Millions of units, a generation reintroduced to tinkering. It wasn't the only thing that was pushed to solve this problem, but it was one of the more prominent things. Physical computing, making an LED flash on and off again, was the new write-your-own video game. Not least because for a brief time in the mid-2010s, kids, or at least young adults, university students, could build or at least prototype IoT devices in their bedroom or dorm room that was just as good as us professionals could, just about. It took years of thinking about the problem before building the solution, and it only worked because the diagnosis was right.

The current AI disruption is the exact same dynamic, but at the professional level, rather than with the kids before they even get to university. The scut work that built engineering teams, bugs, boilerplate, glue code, documentation, is now handled before juniors ever see it. Anthropic's internal data quantifies the new shape of engineering work. Engineers report using Claude in 59% of their daily work, up from 28% a year earlier, achieving a self-reported 50% productivity increase, although do bear in mind the studies I talked about. More than half say they can only fully delegate about 20% of their workload. One engineer estimated that their role had shifted to 70% from being code to being a code reviewer reviser, not a code writer. Claude Code autonomous actions doubled from 9.8 to 21.2 between February and August last year, while human turns per transcript fell from 6.2 to 4.1. The work that remains is increasingly supervisorial, architectural, and judgment heavy.

Now there's evidence that the pipeline isn't just failing to build judgment, it's failing to hire the people who would have built it. At the start of this month, Anthropic published its own labor market analysis, Labor market impacts of AI: A new measure and early evidence. The paper introduces a new measure called observed exposure that combines theoretical LLM capability with actual Claude usage data, weighing automated and work-related uses more heavily. The report makes interesting reading. Computer programmers are the most exposed occupation at 74.5%, meaning three-quarters of our programmers' daily tasks are now seeing significant automated usage in real world professional settings. At the other end, 30% of workers have zero coverage, cooks, mechanics, lifeguards, and bartenders. My parents told me not to become a lifeguard. The physical world remains untouched, at least for now. Although I'm not sure given the opportunity to comment back in the late '90s in the heat of the first dot-com boom before the bust, I would have predicted Uber. Given the effect the internet and the iPhone, which really did change everything, folks, would have on taxi drivers.

AI is far from reaching the theoretical capabilities. The gap between what LLMs could theoretically do and what they actually are being used for remains large. Computer and math occupations have 94% theoretical exposure, but only 33% observed coverage. The red area, actual usage, is much smaller than the blue area, theoretical capability, across every occupational category. This is a modest gap from the start of the talk, measured precisely. Occupations with higher exposure are projected by the Bureau of Labor Statistics in the U.S., the BLS, to grow less through 2034. For every 10 percentage points increase in AI task coverage, the government's growth projection drops by 0.6 percentage points. The labor market analysis is already pricing in job displacement. However, there is no systemic increase in unemployment for highly exposed workers, yet. The unemployment rate for AI-exposed occupations has not diverged from baseline since ChatGPT's release. This is important.

The sky has not fallen. The doom predictors are wrong so far. The hiring of young workers has slowed in exposed occupations. New job stats for 22 to 25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations visibly diverge downwards from unexposed occupations starting in 2024. The average estimate is a 14% drop in job finding rate, not advertisements, finding, in exposed occupations compared to pre-ChatGPT levels. There is no decrease in work for workers over 25. This is just for junior developers just joining the industry. The result is barely statistically significant, but it echoes independent findings from another report based on ADP payroll data. That survey found a 6% to 16% fall in employment in exposed occupations from workers 22 to 25, again, primarily being driven by slowed hiring rather than increased separations. People weren't being sacked. They just weren't being hired in the first place. This is the missing rungs thesis measured in the labor market in real time.

The rungs aren't just losing their educational function, the jobs themselves are contracting. Companies aren't firing experienced workers, they're not hiring the next generation. The pipeline is being cut off at the intake, not the output. Once again, it's Anthropic publishing this. The company that builds Claude is documenting the evidence that Claude's adoption correlates with reduced hiring in young workers in the most exposed professions. Three studies from the same company. AI stunts skill formation, AI transforms work into supervision, and now AI slows hiring at the entry level. Whatever you think of the emotives, their intellectual honesty is remarkable.

Every source, including Anthropic's own internal research, agrees on this. The organization is racing to adopt AI coding tools that are simultaneously degrading the pipeline that produces people capable of supervising those tools. The people who can build good context for AI are people that have the context in their heads through years of craft. The pipeline that produced these people is breaking. If you come here hoping for me to hear an answer to this, I'm really sorry to disappoint you because I don't have one. There are no easy answers because right now I don't have a Raspberry Pi piece shaped to fit the AI shaped hole. There are some things we can do now before we have the answers. Structured learning paths, deliberate rotation through fundamental work, even if the AI could do it faster, like medical residencies. You do the scut work because it teaches you, not because it's efficient.

Require understanding before letting the AI help. It's expensive and most companies won't do it. The ones who do will be the ones who have senior developers left in 10 years because the rest of us will have retired by then. There's a reason why medical doctors are trained how they're trained. Measure understanding, not velocity. Can you explain what code does? Can you debug without AI? What happens if this fails? Pair programming is evaluation. Watch how they think, not what they produce. Treat context as infrastructure. The teams that get AI right are treating codified context as teaching documents, not config files. The best ones read like onboarding docs for a senior engineer who already knows how to code but doesn't know your codebase.

We have entered an era of documentation first programming. I find it somewhat ironic that the part that most developers hate has become the whole job. As an ex-docs person, I'm really happy. Beyond that, we need honest expectations. Tell the juniors that AI is a tool, not a teacher. Using it to skip understanding is borrowing against your future. The people who are valuable in 10 years are the ones who understand the systems, not ones who can prompt. Going back to the original METR study and making wild, waving extrapolations at the current doubling rate, AI agents will handle tasks equivalent to a full work day by 2027, and a work month by 2029. Take that with more than a pinch of salt, mind you, because personally I don't believe we'll ever get there. Even Moore's Law broke eventually. At that point, the middle rungs aren't just missing, the ladder is being rebuilt against a different wall.

If you have 10 bakers producing 1,000 loaves a month and new ovens let 5 of them go on and do the same job, you either cut staff or bake more bread. Anthropic's data offers one possible escape, 27% of Claude-assisted work comes from tasks that would not have been done without AI. New experiments, nice to have tooling, exploratory work, not baking faster, but baking things that weren't previously worth baking. Writing code is a huge bottleneck, was a huge bottleneck. Software started out custom developed in companies for their own use. It became a mass-produced commodity when demand grew past available supply. The entire SaaS market sits on the shoulders of shortage, a shortage of developers, of time to build the perfect custom solution for every problem. Instead, we're stuck with spreadsheets and web applications designed for most cases.

I'll give you a personal example. Over the last few months I've been building a programming language, Vera, designed specifically for AI-generated code, a language designed for machines to write.
